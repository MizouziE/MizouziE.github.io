# Guns, Germs and Steel

It starts with a New Guinean politician asking Diamond why the Europeans turned up with all the cargo, and the rest is a very long answer that refuses at every turn to involve anybody being cleverer than anybody else. The pieces are the crops that happened to grow wild in the Fertile Crescent, the small handful of large animals on earth that can actually be domesticated, and the fact that Eurasia runs east to west so a crop can travel along a latitude, while in the Americas it can't. Dense settled farming then gets you surplus, cities, writing, states, and the diseases that did most of the actual conquering long before anybody fired a gun. The Cajamarca chapter, 168 Spaniards against Atahualpa's army, is the entire thesis playing out in a single afternoon. It gets called determinist a lot, and that criticism isn't nothing.

The perspective gained from this was probably its greatest value, besides the fact it was genuinely interesting and well written. I think reading this is a good cure for any person suffering from delusions of any particular group of humans being in any way superior. We may feel like we controlled how civilisations developed, but we've always been at the mercy of geography, botany, zoology and microbiology.
